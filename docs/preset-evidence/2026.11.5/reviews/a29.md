# A29 — independent complete-patch mallet spectrum and damping review

## Outcome and scope

No blocking material-identity, finite-output or single-note headroom issue was
found in this bounded review. An independent analysis of **16 complete factory
renders** passed **42 explicit conditions**, with zero failures. Xylophone,
Marimba, Steel Drum and Vibraphone retain clearly different tuned-mode balances
at matched sounding C4 and C5. Their held decay and Vibraphone's key damping
also agree with the revised descriptions.

This is a software signal-behavior check. It does not establish a match to a
recording, a particular physical instrument, a listener preference, or native
plugin qualification. No factory control, calibration value, shared build or
production source was modified by this reviewer.

## Independent method

I copied the final-calibrated `build-preset/cs80_preset_capture` executable to a
private directory before rendering, then used its complete factory patches.
All authored rank mixes, filters, variants, effects, touch settings and master
trims remained intact. The commands used 48 kHz stereo float32, velocity 0.85
and zero pressure. No audio normalization or processing was applied.

Each of four programs was rendered at two main-body sounding pitches, with a
held and a released condition:

| Main-body sounding pitch | Xylophone played MIDI note | Other three played MIDI notes |
|---|---:|---:|
| C4 | 48 | 60 |
| C5 | 60 | 72 |

Xylophone's main rank is 4′; the other main ranks are 8′. Matching the sounding
pitch prevents a register difference from masquerading as a material
difference. The separately authored upper ranks remain part of each complete
patch. Steel Drum's octave rank and Vibraphone's fourth-harmonic strike can
therefore contribute to the reported upper-mode energy; this probe does not
attribute all measured energy to the body generator alone.

The held condition lasts 2.2 seconds. The released condition holds for 0.8
seconds and has a 1.4-second release excerpt. Every render contains 105,600
stereo frames; each pair is sample-identical before key-up. All 16 files are
finite and below full scale. The maximum observed sample peak is **−20.08
dBFS**, in the C5 Vibraphone render. This headroom observation is limited to
these single-note gestures; the full-bank polyphonic audit is a separate gate.

Spectra use a Hann window over 15–120 ms, a 262,144-point real FFT, and stereo
RMS spectral amplitude. Local peaks are searched within ±1.8% of multiples of
the measured fundamental. The late spectrum uses 400–700 ms. The generous
search accommodates existing tuning drift and window resolution. All measured
fundamentals remain within 15 cents of the intended sounding pitch. The FFT
zero-padding gives a finer peak-search grid, not additional underlying
frequency resolution.

The independent analysis asserts opposing third/fourth mode balances for
Xylophone/Marimba, opposing octave-plus-twelfth/fourth balances for Steel
Drum/Vibraphone, and at least 40 dB of Vibraphone key damping. The large
numerical separations involving nearly absent modes are used only as guards;
they are not presented as precise physical rejection figures or quality
scores.

## Modal balance and decay

The following are early upper-mode amplitudes relative to each complete
patch's fundamental:

| Existing program | Relevant mode | Sounding C4 | Sounding C5 |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Xylophone | 3× fundamental | −8.92 dB | −11.08 dB |
| Marimba | 4× fundamental | −15.87 dB | −18.16 dB |
| Steel Drum | 2× fundamental | −7.94 dB | −9.21 dB |
| Steel Drum | 3× fundamental | −12.87 dB | −15.28 dB |
| Vibraphone | 4× fundamental | −10.08 dB | −10.52 dB |

Xylophone exposes the strong third harmonic described by its corrected
user-facing text. Marimba instead emphasizes the fourth. Steel Drum contains
the tuned octave and twelfth, while Vibraphone retains its fourth-based bar
and upper-strike spectrum. These distinctions survive the complete authored
signal chain at both tested sounding registers.

The early-to-late stereo RMS change is:

| Existing program | C4: 400–700 ms relative to 15–120 ms | C5: same comparison |
|---|---:|---:|
| Xylophone | −18.11 dB | −20.91 dB |
| Marimba | −10.13 dB | −11.84 dB |
| Steel Drum | −9.36 dB | −10.19 dB |
| Vibraphone | −4.63 dB | −4.92 dB |

The short Xylophone loses energy faster than the fuller Marimba, and the
Vibraphone retains a longer ringing body. The relevant upper/fundamental
balance also changes through the decay: Xylophone's third falls by 25.46/33.27
dB between the early and late windows at C4/C5; Marimba's fourth falls by
16.05/21.35 dB. Steel Drum's twelfth falls by 6.56/7.94 dB, and Vibraphone's
fourth by 14.04/17.65 dB. Thus the measured sounds do not simply retain a fixed
opening spectrum at a lower overall level.

## Key release

For damping, I compared the released and still-held versions at the same
absolute time, 1.10–1.40 seconds after onset. This excludes ordinary modal decay
as the explanation for a released note becoming quieter.

Vibraphone key-up adds **72.86 dB of attenuation at C4** and **72.14 dB at C5**
relative to the otherwise identical held note. The corresponding held
references are approximately −37.15 and −37.13 dBFS RMS, comfortably above the
analysis floor. The prompt damper behavior is therefore well distinguished
from the ordinary ringing decay in these conditions.

The wood and shell voices also attenuate after key-up. Their quieter late
references, especially Xylophone at roughly −79 to −87 dBFS, make their release
ratios less useful as headline evidence. The raw results retain those values
without interpreting them as equivalent physical damper mechanisms. Pedal
behavior is outside this probe and remains covered by the separate Engine and
keyboard reviews.

## Recipe and description checks

The current Xylophone description correctly says **strong third harmonic**;
its two active 4′ ranks select the Xylophone variant and use the truthful
`dual-rank` tag. Steel Drum uses the Steelpan variant and complete body mixes
on both ranks, with its fixed ring carrier disabled. Marimba uses the Standard
Marimba profile, while Vibraphone uses Standard Vibraphone and retains its
5.2 Hz amplitude motion.

Source inspection also confirms that Celesta and Toy Piano remain separately
programmed: both main ranks use the Celesta model, but Toy Piano has a brighter,
shorter main body and a Bell-model upper ping, while Celesta uses a softer,
longer main body and a Celesta-model upper glint. Their descriptions do not
claim sampled or measured instrument identity. This assignment did not render
those two keyboard programs; their keyboard regression and edge checks are
separate evidence.
